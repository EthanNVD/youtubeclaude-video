On the 16th of December, 1944, a German V-weapon fell on a crowded cinema in the Belgian port of Antwerp. Until then, the rockets falling on the city had done surprisingly little harm to soldiers. This one killed two hundred and ninety-six British soldiers.

That same morning, far to the southeast, in the snowy forests of the Ardennes, five German armoured divisions and more than twelve infantry divisions, with around five hundred medium tanks and nineteen hundred guns and rocket launchers, crashed into a thinly held stretch of the American line. Their final objective was the same city. Antwerp.

For a few days, it looked as if the war in the west had turned upside down. American divisions were cut off. Columns of Germans raced west through the fog. And the Allies, who had been arguing about how to finish the war by spring, suddenly found themselves arguing about how to stop a German army they had believed was finished.

Afterwards, everyone had an explanation. American intelligence had been asleep. The Ardennes front had been left dangerously thin. The weather had grounded the Allied air forces. And on the German side, the explanation was simpler. Hitler did it. His own field marshals had tried to talk him into something smaller.

In late September, Jodl's staff began turning the idea into a plan. The commander in the west, Field Marshal Gerd von Rundstedt, wasn't even told about it at first. The staff studied five possible courses. On the 9th of October, Hitler chose the most ambitious, a huge double envelopment that would trap the Allied armies north of a line from Bastogne through Brussels to Antwerp. The German planners had a card-table name for it. They called it the Grand Slam, or the Big Solution.

On the 22nd of October, the chiefs of staff of Rundstedt and of Field Marshal Walter Model, who would command the attack, arrived at the Wolf's Lair. In December 1944, the American front in the Ardennes was held by Major General Troy Middleton's VIII Corps. It was a quiet sector, and it was meant to be. Two of Middleton's infantry divisions, the 28th and the 4th, had been badly mauled in the Hürtgen Forest in November, with some nine thousand battle casualties between them. They had been sent to the Ardennes to rest, train replacements and watch the Germans. The third, the 106th, had just arrived from the United States. It had only finished taking over its positions on the high ridge called the Schnee Eifel on the 12th of December, four days before the attack.

Those three divisions, with a cavalry group and part of a green armoured division, held a front of about eighty-five miles. The official American history points out that this was roughly three times what American doctrine said a force that size should defend. On the morning of the 16th, the whole corps numbered just under sixty-nine thousand men.

The point of the Sixth Panzer Army's attack was an armoured battle group from the 1st SS Panzer Division under Colonel Joachim Peiper. His orders were to race west to the Meuse. The town that mattered most in the south was Bastogne, where the roads of the southern Ardennes came together. On the morning of the 19th, Middleton gave the commander of the 101st Airborne Division's troops there, Brigadier General Anthony McAuliffe, a one-line order. Hold Bastogne. Within days, the town was surrounded. The fog and cloud kept Allied planes from dropping supplies.

On the 22nd of December, the Germans sent in a written demand for surrender, threatening annihilation by their massed artillery. McAuliffe's reply has become legend. Nuts. One of his regimental commanders, asked to explain the word to the German officers, settled for go to hell.

Now, to be fair, not everyone agrees on how mad the bet was. Rundstedt, of all people, called the idea almost a stroke of genius. Hitler's own argument was that a quick success might split the alliance and push the West toward a separate peace, and in a war Germany was already losing slowly, a gamble was the only way left to change the odds. Against that, even the Small Solution would have burned many of the same reserves for a much smaller prize. Even victory caused arguments.
