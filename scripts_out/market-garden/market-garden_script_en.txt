To understand why anyone thought this could work, start with the summer that made it look easy.

In August 1944, the German army in France fell apart. After the breakout from Normandy and the pocket at Falaise, the Allies chased it across France and Belgium faster than their own maps could keep up. Between early June and the middle of August, the Germans in the west had lost nearly two hundred thousand men missing or captured, on top of the dead and wounded. On the 4th of September, British tanks rolled into Antwerp, one of the biggest ports in Europe, and found its docks almost intact.

In Allied headquarters, the mood was something close to a fever. The Supreme Allied Command's intelligence staff counted forty-eight German divisions in the west, and reckoned they were worth about twenty infantry divisions and four armoured ones. A British airborne staff calculated that the whole of the Netherlands held no more than fifty to a hundred German tanks. Looking back afterwards, the British airborne division admitted what it had assumed: an enemy still disorganised after a long, hasty retreat, scattered in small groups, and not capable of much organised resistance.

There was just one problem, and it was the oldest one in war. Fuel. The petrol was sitting in Normandy, hundreds of kilometres behind the front, and there weren't enough trucks to bring it forward. Antwerp could have fixed that. Its docks were in Allied hands. But the long estuary that connects Antwerp to the sea, the Scheldt, was still held by the Germans on both banks. A port you can't sail into is just a very large car park.

The admirals saw it at once. Admiral Andrew Cunningham warned that unless the approaches were cleared, Antwerp would be about as much use as Timbuktu. And while the Allied armies looked east toward Germany, a whole German army, the Fifteenth, was quietly escaping across that estuary behind them. The Germans were doing something else, too. Along the Albert Canal in Belgium, a general named Kurt Chill, retreating with the remains of three divisions, simply stopped. Here's our verdict. The defeat at Arnhem was avoidable, but not by one heroic decision. The plan needed every bridge, every day and every radio to work, and it had no spare time built in for the first thing that didn't. Now, to be fair, historians don't agree on where the weight lies. The American official history, written in 1963, found the plan itself drew little criticism, and argued that its handicaps could have been overcome if the ground column had moved as fast as Horrocks hoped. Many later writers put the blame squarely on the plan: the distant drop zones, the single daily lift, the radios. Others stress how fast the Germans recovered, how a beaten army rebuilt what one British historian calls a crust, out of stragglers and training battalions, in less than two weeks. And Montgomery, who called it ninety percent successful, later admitted something closer to the truth.
